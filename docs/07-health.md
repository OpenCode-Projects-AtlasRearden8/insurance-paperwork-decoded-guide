# Health Policies, Translated

> **Education only.** This guide explains health-insurance *paperwork*, not
> medicine, not benefits decisions, and not what plan to choose. For
> Medicare-specific reading, see the companion project
> *medicare-decoded-guide*.

Health coverage has its own skeleton and its own vocabulary. The good news:
the vocabulary is standardized across most plans, so learning it once
transfers everywhere.

## The documents you'll actually hold

- **Summary of Benefits and Coverage (SBC):** a standardized, plain-language
  summary every plan must provide. Short tables of coverage examples. Start
  here for everyday questions.
- **Plan brochure / certificate of coverage:** the full contract. Longer,
  but organized the same way every time (below).
- **Evidence of Coverage (EOC):** the Medicare-Advantage-style name for the
  full contract. Same idea.
- **Explanations of Benefits (EOB):** *not* a bill — a per-claim report of
  what the provider charged, what the plan allowed, what the plan paid, and
  what's left for you. Decoded below.

## The plan document's skeleton

1. **Covered benefits** — each service category (office visits, imaging,
   prescriptions, mental health) with its cost-sharing row: copay,
   coinsurance, deductible applicability.
2. **Medical necessity and prior authorization** — the rules for when a
   service needs pre-approval. This section is the one denial letters
   quote most often, so note where it lives.
3. **Networks** — in-network vs. out-of-network cost sharing, and how
   emergency care is handled. The network list itself is usually a separate
   directory.
4. **Exclusions and limitations** — commonly: cosmetic services, some
   dental/vision, experimental or investigational treatments (a *defined*
   term — check the definitions).
5. **Claims and appeals** — deadlines and levels of appeal. Flag this
   section the moment you enroll; guide 10 leans on it.

## The EOB, line by line

An EOB is arithmetic in costume. The columns, translated:

- **Billed amount** — the provider's sticker price. Largely irrelevant to
  you.
- **Allowed amount (allowed charge)** — the contracted rate the plan
  recognizes. *This* is the number your cost-sharing is computed from.
- **Plan paid** — the insurer's share.
- **Patient responsibility** — your share (deductible, copay, coinsurance).
- **Not covered / denied** — the amount neither party pays, with a reason
  code.

**Worked example (invented, round):** office visit, billed $400, allowed
$150, plan paid $100, patient responsibility $50.

- The $50 is 33% of the allowed amount — consistent with a plan whose
  cost-sharing after deductible is… well, whatever your SBC says. The skill
  is checking the EOB's arithmetic *against your plan documents*, line by
  line, and flagging mismatches rather than trusting either document alone.

## The year in health-paperwork terms

- **Plan year** (often but not always the calendar year): deductibles and
  out-of-pocket maximums reset. January paperwork is heavier than it looks.
- **Open enrollment:** the window to change plans for the next year.
- **Annual Notice of Change (Medicare Advantage):** what's changing next
  year — covered in the companion medicare guide.

## Coordination of benefits

If two plans could apply (yours and a spouse's), coordination-of-benefits
rules decide which pays first. The paperwork asks you to confirm this
yearly — answering it accurately prevents the classic "both plans denied as
secondary" loop.

## The appeal scaffolding (preview)

Every plan document contains a mandated appeals process with deadlines:
internal appeals first (usually a stated number of days to file, and
stated timelines for the plan to answer), then external review by an
independent reviewer. Guide 10 walks the first response. What this guide
contributes: *find the deadlines in your plan document now, while nothing
is wrong, and write them on Worksheet E.* Deadlines are infinitely easier
to meet when you look them up on a calm day.

## The annual health check

During the guide-11 ritual: did the network directory change, did any
prescription move tiers, are your primary-care and pharmacy still
in-network, and are the appeal deadlines still written on Worksheet E?
