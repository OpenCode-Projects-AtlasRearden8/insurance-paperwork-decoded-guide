# Denial Letters: The First Response

> **Education only.** This guide explains what denial letters generally
> contain and how first responses are generally structured. It is not legal
> advice and never tells you whether your specific denial is right.

A denial letter is not a verdict. It is a *first offer of an explanation*,
and its most important feature is that it must state its reasons. Your
first response is a paperwork project, like a claim — and it starts the
same way: calmly, with a checklist.

## What a denial letter must give you

Read the letter for these five items. Most letters contain all five;
noting which one is missing is itself useful.

1. **The decision,** in one sentence ("the claim is denied").
2. **The reason,** usually quoting a policy provision or plan clause.
3. **The cited language,** often verbatim — a section number or a quoted
   sentence.
4. **Your appeal rights,** including deadlines and where to send appeals.
5. **What would change the outcome,** sometimes stated explicitly ("submit
   documentation of…").

## The first-response checklist (48 hours, all calm)

- [ ] Write the **appeal deadline** on Worksheet E the day the letter
      arrives. Everything else can wait; the deadline cannot.
- [ ] Read the cited clause in *your own* policy or plan document. Not the
      letter's summary — the actual clause. (Guide 01's three lookups:
      defined term, insuring agreement, exclusion.)
- [ ] Sort the reason into one of four buckets (below).
- [ ] Gather the documents the letter says would change the outcome.
- [ ] Note every phone call: date, name, what was said.

## The four buckets of denial reasons

Almost every denial sorts into one of these. The bucket tells you what
kind of response the situation generally calls for — this is structure,
not advice.

### Bucket 1: Administrative or coding

Wrong code, missing pre-authorization, service submitted under the wrong
provider, date mismatches. These are paperwork artifacts. The general
shape of a response: a call to the provider's billing office and/or the
insurer to correct and resubmit. Many denials in this bucket never become
disputes at all.

### Bucket 2: Coverage exclusion

The letter says the item falls under an exclusion. The general shape of a
response: read the exclusion's *definitions* closely (guide 01's micro-
example method). If the facts genuinely match the defined term, the
response is a different question — one for your agent, your state
insurance department's consumer office, or an attorney. If the facts are
arguably outside the defined term, that's what an appeal letter documents.

### Bucket 3: Medical necessity or authorization (health)

The plan says the service wasn't medically necessary or wasn't authorized.
The general shape: your provider is the key participant — necessity
determinations rest on clinical judgment, and appeals in this bucket are
typically built from the provider's records and a letter from the
provider. Your role is logistics: deadlines, forms, assembling what the
provider supplies.

### Bucket 4: Eligibility, timing, or limits

Out-of-network, out-of-policy-period, above a limit, deductible not
arranged as assumed. The general shape: verify the facts against the dec
page and plan documents. Some of these are genuine mismatches; some are
data errors (a policy period misread, a network file that's out of date).
Verification is cheap; do it before anything else.

## The appeal letter skeleton

If you appeal, the letter structure that serves every bucket:

1. **Header:** policy/claim numbers, date of denial letter, one-line
   statement of what you're appealing.
2. **The facts,** dated, in order, one paragraph. No adjectives.
3. **The question:** what specifically you're asking them to reconsider,
   tied to the reason they gave.
4. **The documents,** listed by name and enclosed.
5. **The ask:** the specific outcome requested, and a request for a written
   response.

Keep the whole thing under one page plus enclosures. Keep a copy. Send it
the way the letter says to send appeals (some require specific addresses
or portals — the appeal-rights section states it).

## Escalation paths that exist (structure only)

If an internal appeal doesn't resolve it, structures that exist in most
situations include: your state insurance department's consumer complaint
process (for regulated insurance lines), external/independent review (for
many health denials, after internal appeal), and — for genuinely disputed
contract matters — professional advice. Which path fits your situation is
exactly the kind of question for the consumer office or a professional;
this manual's job ends at making the paperwork legible enough to describe
your situation to them in one paragraph.

That one paragraph is worth preparing regardless: *policy number, claim
number, what happened, what was denied, the stated reason, the deadline.*
Six facts. With those, any helper can help you fast.

## The emotional note (brief, on purpose)

Denial letters are written in a register that reads as final. It isn't
personal and it isn't final — it's a form letter generated at a decision
point, with appeal rights attached because the system expects appeals. Put
the letter in the binder, write the deadline down, close the folder, and
let it be tomorrow's project. That's a complete first response.
