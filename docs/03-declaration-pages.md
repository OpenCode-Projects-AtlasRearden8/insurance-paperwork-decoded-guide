# The Declarations Page, Decoded

> **Education only.** The declarations page in *your* policy controls what
> *your* coverage says. This guide teaches you what each line *is*, not what
> yours should say.

The declarations page ("dec page") is the one- to three-page summary at the
front of your policy. It's the single highest-value page in the envelope:
every number you need day-to-day lives here. This guide walks it top to
bottom. Generic labels are used so the guide matches any carrier's layout.

## Line-by-line tour

### 1. Policy number and policy period

- **Policy number:** your ID with the carrier. Every call, claim, and email
  starts with this.
- **Policy period:** the dates you're covered, e.g. `12/01/2026 –
  12/01/2027`. Note the renewal date — the annual review ritual (guide 11)
  anchors to it.

### 2. Named insured(s) and mailing address

The person(s) the policy covers by name. Check spelling, check the address,
check that everyone who should be a named insured is one. For auto, listed
drivers usually appear nearby too.

### 3. Location / property / vehicle descriptions

What's being insured: the car's VIN, the rental unit's address, the house
address, or (life) just you. Verify the VIN and address carefully — a stale
address on the dec page is the classic paperwork leak.

### 4. Coverage parts and limits

A table. Each row is a coverage; each row has a **limit** (the most it
pays). Typical rows:

- *Auto:* bodily injury per person/per accident, property damage,
  collision, comprehensive, uninsured motorist, medical payments/PIP,
  roadside, rental reimbursement.
- *Homeowners:* dwelling (Coverage A), other structures (B), personal
  property (C), loss of use (D), personal liability (E), medical payments
  to others (F).
- *Renters:* personal property (C), loss of use (D), liability (E),
  medical payments to others (F) — no dwelling coverage, because you don't
  own the building.
- *Life:* the face amount (the death benefit), the premium, and the policy
  type (term or permanent).

Read the limits as dollar ceilings. Under each, the deductible (if any)
appears — sometimes as a separate column, sometimes in a footnote.

### 5. Deductibles

Listed per coverage. Look for the wrinkles from guide 02: separate
wind/hail or hurricane deductibles, often shown as a percentage.

### 6. Premium

The total price, usually broken out by coverage line and shown as
 six-month, yearly, or monthly. The breakdown tells you what each coverage
actually costs — useful at renewal when you're deciding what to keep.

### 7. Endorsements and forms list

A list of form numbers attached to the policy (guide 01, part 6). If this
list is longer than you remember, something changed at renewal — each form
number can be looked up in the packet that came with the renewal.

### 8. Discounts and surcharges

Bundling, alarm, claims-free, auto-pay. Worth a skim once a year: discounts
expire quietly (an alarm monitoring lapse, a telematics program ending).

### 9. Loss payees / mortgagees / beneficiaries

If a lender has an interest in the property (mortgage, auto loan), their
name appears here. Life policies name beneficiaries here or on a separate
beneficiary page — **check that the names are current.** This is one of the
most commonly stale lines on any dec page.

## The five-number exercise

Take any dec page and write down five numbers (Worksheet A has slots):

1. Premium (and its period)
2. Deductible (each one, if several)
3. Coverage limit(s) — at minimum the biggest one
4. Policy period end date
5. The claim phone number (usually printed in the header or footer)

Five numbers, ten minutes, and you now know your policy better than most
people ever will.

## Red flags worth a phone call

These are *structural* observations, not advice — but each one is worth a
call to your agent or carrier to ask about:

- A named insured who moved out or died, still listed.
- A vehicle or address you don't recognize.
- An endorsement list that grew at the last renewal.
- A deductible that changed shape (flat dollar → percentage).
- A limit that hasn't moved in years while everything else has.

A dec page read once a year, with five numbers written down, is the entire
maintenance this page needs.
