# Renters Policies, Translated

> **Education only.** Your lease and your policy control. This guide
> explains what renters-policy sections *are*, not what you should carry.

Renters insurance is the shortest policy in this manual — often under 20
pages — which makes it the best one to practice the three-pass method from
guide 01 on. It has no Coverage A (you don't own the building); everything
orbits your personal property and your liability.

## The coverage rows

### Coverage C — Personal property

Your stuff: furniture, clothes, electronics, kitchenware, books. Two
mechanics to understand:

- **Actual cash value (ACV) vs. replacement cost (RCV).** ACV pays what
  the item is worth *used* (a 5-year-old TV at 5-year-old-TV prices). RCV
  pays what a new one costs, usually after you actually replace the item
  and submit receipts. The dec page states which one you have; it's
  usually on the dec page in plain words.
- **Special limits on categories.** Policies cap certain categories —
  jewelry, watches, firearms, cash, business property, sometimes
  electronics — at flat dollar amounts per item or per category (e.g.
  "$1,500 in all"). If you own anything above those caps, policies offer a
  **scheduled personal property** endorsement to list items individually.
  Whether to do that is a decision for you and your agent; the *reading*
  skill is just noticing the caps exist.

### Coverage D — Loss of use

Pays extra living costs if the unit becomes unlivable — a hotel, food
above your normal grocery bill, laundry. Usually a limit like "$3,000" or
"20% of Coverage C," sometimes with a time cap. Keep receipts; this
coverage is receipt-driven.

### Coverage E — Personal liability

Pays when you're legally responsible for injury or property damage to
others — the classic example being a guest's fall — up to the limit.
Includes the insurer's duty to defend (lawyer costs) which often matters
more than the limit itself.

### Coverage F — Medical payments to others

Small flat-limit coverage (often $1,000–$5,000) paying guests' minor
medical bills regardless of fault, to keep small incidents small.

## The exclusions that matter in rentals

Headlines to skim for: floods and earthquakes (separate policies exist),
pests (bedbugs are almost universally excluded — leases usually assign
responsibility instead), roommate property (policies generally cover the
named insured; a roommate needs their own policy), and business equipment
beyond a small cap.

## Landlord vs. tenant: who insures what

A rule of thumb for reading your situation: the landlord's policy insures
the *building and its fixtures*; your policy insures *your property* and
*your liability*. Anything bolted in (fridge that came with the unit,
smoke detectors) is generally theirs; anything you could carry out the
door is generally yours. Gray zones (your own washer/dryer, improvements
you installed) are exactly what to ask your agent about — in writing.

## Roommates

Most policies cover the named insured and resident relatives. A
non-relative roommate is usually *not* covered by your policy and their
property is *not* your claim. Two roommates usually means two policies.
Read the "Who Is An Insured" definition — it's the whole answer.

## A claim-shaped mental model

- Laptop stolen from the apartment → Coverage C, subject to the theft
  deductible and the electronics special limit.
- Kitchen fire (yours) scorches the counter → your liability (E) for the
  landlord's damage; your own scorched pans are Coverage C.
- Pipe floods the building and you're displaced a month → Coverage D,
  receipts required.
- Guest trips over your rug → Coverage F for the small bill, E if it
  becomes a claim.

## The annual renters check

During the guide-11 ritual: has your stuff's value drifted up (new
computer, bike, instruments)? Are the category caps still above what you
own in those categories? Is the roommate situation the same as the policy
assumes? Three questions, five minutes.
