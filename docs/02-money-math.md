# Premiums, Deductibles, and Out-of-Pocket: Plain Arithmetic

> **Education only.** This guide explains how cost-sharing words work in
> general, with invented round numbers. It is not pricing advice, and no
> example describes any real product.

Three words do almost all the work in insurance money math. This guide gives
each one a one-line job description, then works the arithmetic until it's
boring.

## The three words, in one line each

- **Premium** — what you pay to *keep* the policy, whether or not anything
  ever happens.
- **Deductible** — what you pay *first* on a covered claim, before the
  insurer pays anything.
- **Out-of-pocket maximum (or out-of-pocket cost)** — in health insurance,
  the yearly ceiling on your share; everywhere else, just shorthand for
  "the total you actually paid."

## 1. Premium: the price of the card in your wallet

A premium is rent for protection. You pay it monthly, quarterly, or yearly.
If nothing ever goes wrong, the premium bought you the *state of being
covered* — which is a real thing, even when it feels invisible.

**Arithmetic:** a policy with a $1,200 yearly premium costs $100 a month.
Over five years that's $6,000 paid in premiums, whether you filed zero
claims or nine.

Two premium facts worth knowing:

- Premiums change at renewal. The renewal notice will state the new amount.
- Paying monthly sometimes costs slightly more than paying yearly (a
  financing fee). The dec page or billing notice shows both if so.

## 2. Deductible: the first dollars of a claim

A deductible is the part of a *covered loss* you absorb before insurance
contributes. It applies **per claim** in property insurance (auto, home,
renters) and **per year** in most health plans.

**Arithmetic (property style):** your deductible is $500. A covered repair
costs $2,300.

- You pay the first $500.
- The insurer pays the remaining $1,800.

**Arithmetic (health style):** your yearly deductible is $1,500. In
February you have $800 of covered services — you pay all $800 (deductible
not yet met). In March, another $900. The first $700 of that finishes the
deductible; the remaining $200 is now subject to whatever comes after the
deductible (copays or coinsurance — below).

Watch for two wrinkles:

- **Some coverages carry separate deductibles.** A homeowners policy often
  has one deductible for most perils and a separate (usually percentage)
  deductible for wind/hail or hurricane. The dec page lists each one.
- **Percentage deductibles are a percent of the insured amount,** not of
  the loss. 2% of a $200,000 dwelling limit = a $4,000 deductible.

## 3. Copays and coinsurance: the middle layer (mostly health)

After the deductible, health plans usually split costs one of two ways:

- **Copay** — a flat amount per service. "$25 per office visit" means $25,
  regardless of the visit's cost.
- **Coinsurance** — a percentage split. "20% coinsurance" means after the
  deductible you pay 20% of the allowed amount and the plan pays 80%.

**Arithmetic:** deductible met, allowed amount $1,000, plan is 80/20.

- Your share: 20% × $1,000 = $200.
- Plan's share: $800.

## 4. Out-of-pocket maximum: the yearly ceiling (health)

The out-of-pocket maximum is the most you can pay in allowed costs in a plan
year — deductibles, copays, and coinsurance all count toward it (premiums do
*not*). After you reach it, the plan pays 100% of covered in-network services
for the rest of the year.

**Arithmetic:** out-of-pocket maximum $6,000. By August you've paid $4,000
in deductible + copays + coinsurance. A $5,000 allowed hospital stay lands:
you pay the first $2,000 (reaching $6,000), the plan pays the remaining
$3,000, and everything covered for the rest of the year costs you $0.

## 5. Limits: the ceiling per claim (property & life)

Property and life policies don't use out-of-pocket maximums; they use
**limits** — the most the policy will pay for a covered loss. Anything above
the limit is yours to cover.

**Arithmetic:** dwelling limit $200,000, covered repair estimate $215,000.
The policy pays up to $200,000 (minus your deductible); the gap is $15,000
minus whatever your deductible was. This is why the dec page's limits
deserve an annual look — guide 11 builds it into the ritual.

## 6. One worked scenario, end to end

Invented numbers, property style:

- Premium: $600/year. Deductible: $1,000. Covered loss: $9,000.
- You pay $600 in premium that year regardless.
- The claim: you pay the first $1,000; the insurer pays $8,000.
- Your total cost of the year: $600 + $1,000 = **$1,600**.

Same scenario, but the loss is $900 — *below* your deductible:

- You pay $900; the insurer pays $0. No claim is worth filing.
- Total cost of the year: $600 + $900 = **$1,500**.

This is the whole relationship in two pictures: the deductible is the
small-loss line. Below it, you're on your own by design; above it, the
insurer carries the rest up to the limit.

## Vocabulary check

| Word | Applies to | Rhythm | One-line job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Premium | All insurance | Per month/year | Buys the coverage itself |
| Deductible | Property: per claim. Health: per year | First dollars | Your share before the insurer pays |
| Copay | Mostly health | Per service | Flat fee per visit/item |
| Coinsurance | Mostly health | Per service, after deductible | Percentage split |
| Out-of-pocket max | Health | Per year | Ceiling on your yearly share |
| Limit | Property & life | Per claim or per category | Ceiling on what the policy pays |

If those six rows make sense, you can read any dec page's money columns.
That's the whole lesson.
