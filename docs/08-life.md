# Life Policies, Translated

> **Education only.** This guide explains what life-policy sections *are*.
> It never recommends amounts, products, or beneficiaries — those are
> decisions for you and licensed professionals you trust.

Life policies are structurally the simplest in this manual: one insuring
agreement, one big exclusion clause, and a handful of options. The paperwork
complexity lives in *ownership* and *beneficiaries*, not in coverage rows.

## Term vs. permanent, in structure terms

- **Term life:** covers a stated period (10, 20, 30 years). Premium is
  level for the term (or increases on a schedule the dec page shows). When
  the term ends, coverage ends. No cash value. The policy is short — often
  under 30 pages — and the dec page carries almost everything.
- **Permanent life (whole life, universal life, and relatives):** covers
  for life as long as premiums are paid, and includes a **cash value**
  component — a savings-like element that grows per the policy's interest
  or indexing terms. This adds whole sections: cash-value tables, loan
  provisions, surrender charges.

The reading consequence: a term policy is read once with the three-pass
method and revisited rarely. A permanent policy's cash-value sections are
worth an annual look during the guide-11 ritual.

## The sections you'll find

### Declarations / policy data page

Insured's name, policy number, **face amount** (the death benefit), policy
type, issue date, premium amount and frequency, and often the original
beneficiary designation.

### Insuring agreement

One sentence: pay the face amount to the beneficiary upon proof of the
insured's death while the policy is in force. The qualifiers ("in force,"
"proof") point to the conditions section.

### The two-year rules (incontestability and suicide)

Nearly every policy contains two clauses you should know exist:

- **Incontestability:** after the policy has been in force (commonly) two
  years, the insurer generally can't contest statements on the application.
  Within that window, misstatements matter much more.
- **Suicide clause:** death by suicide within (commonly) the first two
  years is excluded or limited to premiums paid.

These are described here as *structure to look for*, not as legal
interpretation — the exact wording in your policy is what applies.

### Grace period and reinstatement

A stated window (commonly 30–31 days) after a missed premium during which
coverage continues; reinstatement rules for policies that lapsed. Note the
numbers on Worksheet A.

### Riders

Common ones: **accelerated death benefit** (access to part of the benefit
on certain qualifying events), **waiver of premium** (premiums waived on
disability, per the rider's definition), **child/term riders**, and
**guaranteed insurability**. Each rider is a short form with its own
definitions — the first paragraph states what it changes.

### Loans and surrenders (permanent policies only)

Sections describing policy loans against cash value, interest on them, and
what happens to the death benefit if a loan is unpaid; and surrender
values/charges if you end the policy. Read these *before* you ever need
them; they interact in surprising ways (an unpaid loan reduces the benefit
dollar-for-dollar in many policies).

## Beneficiary designations: the paperwork heart of the policy

The beneficiary page (sometimes part of the application, sometimes a
separate form) controls who is paid — and it overrides wills in most
situations. Reading skills, not advice:

- **Primary vs. contingent (secondary) beneficiaries:** the contingent
  layer receives only if primaries predecease.
- **Per stirpes vs. per capita:** what happens when a named beneficiary
  has died. The definitions are in the policy; the practical skill is
  knowing the designation has a stated answer either way.
- **Minors as beneficiaries:** policies often require a custodial
  arrangement; the application explains the options.
- **Life changes = designation review.** Marriage, divorce, births,
  deaths. The annual ritual (guide 11) includes checking this page every
  year, because it's the line most likely to be stale.

## A claim-shaped mental model (for survivors)

When a death occurs, the beneficiary's paperwork is: certified death
certificate (usually multiple copies), the policy number or the dec page,
and a claim form from the insurer. Insurers pay lump sums by default and
offer settlement options (interest-bearing accounts, annuitization) — the
options are described in the policy's "settlement options" section. Nothing
needs deciding in the first days; the claim itself doesn't expire quickly.
Calm first, paperwork second.

## The annual life check

During the guide-11 ritual: beneficiaries current? Premiums on
auto-pay and current? For permanent policies: cash-value statement read,
loan balance noted? Term policy: do you know the term's end date?
