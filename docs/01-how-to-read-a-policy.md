# How to Read a Policy, Section by Section

> **Education only.** This guide explains the *structure* of insurance
> policies in general. Your policy's exact wording controls. For questions
> about what your policy means, ask your carrier in writing, your state
> insurance department, or a licensed professional.

Almost every insurance policy — auto, renters, homeowners, life — is built
from the same seven parts. Health policies use a different skeleton (covered
in guide 07), but the reading skill is the same: find the part, read only
that part, ignore the rest until you need it.

## The seven parts

### 1. Declarations page ("dec page")

The summary. Who is insured, what is insured, the coverages, the limits, the
deductibles, the premium, the policy period. Guide 03 decodes it line by
line. **Always read this first.** It answers 80% of everyday questions.

### 2. Definitions

The dictionary. Terms in **bold** or "quotes" throughout the policy are
defined here, and they often mean something narrower than everyday usage.
Example: everyday "flood" means any water coming in. In a homeowners policy,
"Flood" is usually a defined term that means specifically rising surface
water — and it's excluded. The definitions section is where the policy's
real boundaries get drawn.

**Reading habit:** whenever a capitalized or bolded term confuses you, look
it up here before anywhere else.

### 3. Insuring agreement

The promise. One or two sentences: "We will pay for sudden accidental damage
to covered property, up to the limit, subject to the terms of this policy."
Everything else in the policy modifies this promise. If you read only one
sentence of the whole document, read this one — then read it a second time
noticing every qualifier ("covered," "up to the limit," "subject to").

### 4. Exclusions

The exceptions. What the insuring agreement does *not* pay for. Common
categories: wear and tear, intentional acts, business activity, certain
perils (flood, earthquake), certain property types (motor vehicles on a
homeowners policy). Exclusions are usually the longest-looking section, but
you only need the ones relevant to your life. Skim for headlines first.

### 5. Conditions

The rulebook. Your duties and the insurer's duties: when to report a claim,
how to protect property from further damage, proof-of-loss requirements,
subrogation (the insurer's right to pursue whoever caused the loss),
cancellation and non-renewal rules. Deadlines live here. **This is the
section worth flagging with sticky notes** — especially "Duties After
Loss."

### 6. Endorsements (riders)

The amendments. Pages that add, remove, or change coverage, numbered like
"HO-0490" or "Form CA-2001." Endorsements override the base policy text, and
they're attached at the end but apply everywhere. If the base policy and an
endorsement disagree, the endorsement usually wins. Check your dec page for
a list of which endorsements are attached — then skim each one's first
paragraph, which states what it changes.

### 7. Application and signatures page

What you told the insurer when you applied. Worth a skim once a year:
misstatements on applications can cause problems later, so if something is
wrong (old address, wrong mileage estimate), correcting it now is easier.

## Health policies are different

Health coverage uses its own skeleton — Summary of Benefits, covered
benefits, medical-necessity rules, prior authorization, networks, appeals
process. Guide 07 translates that structure. The reading skill transfers:
find the section, read only that section.

## The three-pass method

Don't read a policy front to back. Read it in three passes:

1. **Pass 1 — the dec page (10 minutes).** Names, dates, limits,
   deductibles, premium. Write the numbers down using Worksheet A.
2. **Pass 2 — the insuring agreement + exclusion headlines (30 minutes).**
   What's promised, and the headlines of what isn't. Don't read exclusion
   fine print yet — just the bolded category names.
3. **Pass 3 — conditions and deadlines (20 minutes, once a year).** Your
   duties after a loss, claim-reporting deadlines, cancellation rules.
   Flag them.

Total: about an hour, once, per policy. After that, you only ever return to
one section at a time, when a real question shows up.

## A worked micro-example

Suppose the policy says: "We cover sudden and accidental water damage. We do
not cover damage from flood." A pipe bursts — is that covered?

- **Definitions:** "Flood" is defined as surface water, waves, or water
  below the surface of the ground.
- **Insuring agreement:** sudden and accidental water damage is covered.
- **Exclusions:** only the *defined* term "flood" is excluded.

A burst pipe is sudden, accidental, and not surface water — so by the
structure of the document, it lands on the covered side. (Whether *your*
policy says exactly this is exactly why we read your definitions section,
not this guide.) The method: defined term → insuring agreement → exclusion.
Three lookups, one answer, no guessing.

## What to write down

After your three passes, fill in Worksheet A (Policy Snapshot) in
`docs/worksheets/`. If you only ever fill in one worksheet, make it that one.
